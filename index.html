01 · Historical background

Greece Mythology: where the myths began

Greek myths are stories people told to make sense of nature, life, and the unknown. Gods and heroes stand at the heart of these tales.

Cities, gods, and stories

Many cities, many traditions

Ancient Greece was not a unified country ruled from one capital. Communities lived in independent city-states around the Aegean Sea. Each polis had its own government and laws. Athens, Sparta, and Corinth are well-known examples.

Myths passed from generation to generation through temples, festivals, and oral storytelling. People used these tales to explain thunder, the sea, the seasons, and emotions such as courage and jealousy. Over time, works such as the epic poems attributed to Homer recorded some of these stories in writing.

Today, we read myths not as historical records, but as stories that reflect the beliefs, values, and imagination of their time.

Meet the immortals and heroes

Zeus was the leader of the Olympian gods and was associated with the sky and thunder. Athena was the goddess of wisdom and strategy, and the protector of Athens. Heracles became famous for his strength and his legendary labors. Odysseus, king of Ithaca, was remembered for his long journey and cleverness.

These characters do not appear in only one kind of story. Their adventures explore pride, loyalty, courage, hospitality, and the consequences of difficult choices.

03 · From the past to the present

How do myths live on today?

Ancient stories reach us in many forms. Looking separately at what a story meant in the ancient world and how it is interpreted today helps us understand its cultural context.

Old stories, new perspectives
A modern interpretation

Old stories, new questions

Today, mythological characters reappear in books, theatre, films, games, and works of art. Every retelling brings the questions of its own time to an ancient story. A hero’s journey, for example, can be read not only as a battle with monsters, but also as a story about returning home, finding belonging, or making the right choice.

When comparing different versions of a story, we can ask: “Who is telling it?”, “Whose voice is heard?”, and “What does the character’s choice tell us?” Mythology can help us explore not only the past, but also how we think today.

06 · Turkish mythology and Turkic storytelling

A broad heritage carried by stories

Turkish mythology is connected to a wider family of Turkic traditions. These stories changed across regions and centuries, so there is no single version shared by every community.

TengriEpic poetryDede KorkutManas

Early Turkic worldviews

Some early Turkic worldviews connected human life with the sky, the natural world, ancestors, and the order of the community. The name Tengri appears in the Orkhon inscriptions, eighth-century monuments associated with the early Turkic polities. These inscriptions are historical sources, not a complete record of mythology, but they offer clues about political life and ideas of order, rule, and the sky.

Epic stories and their storytellers

Epic narratives were kept alive through oral transmission, performance, music, and later manuscripts. The Dede Qorqud / Dede Korkut heritage includes twelve heroic tales and traditional music. A manuscript was written down in the fifteenth century, while the stories reflect a longer tradition. UNESCO recognizes this as shared heritage of Türkiye, Azerbaijan, and Kazakhstan.

The Manas trilogy belongs specifically to Kyrgyz epic tradition. Its performance is sustained by skilled narrators, who learn and pass on the stories through a master-and-apprentice tradition. Including it in a broader Turkic studies project can help students compare related traditions while respecting their distinct cultural homes.

Stories in the present

Today, epic traditions continue through performances, music, books, education, and community events. Retellings can bring old characters to new readers, but each version should identify its people, region, and source. In this project, a story can be paired with a short context note, an English reading, audio narration, and a map of where the tradition comes from.

Reading the tradition · Themes, voices, and versions

What can a story carry?

More than a heroic adventure

Epic tales can preserve ideas about family responsibility, courage, hospitality, leadership, and respect for the natural world. They also show moments of conflict: a character may need to protect a community, keep a promise, or choose between personal wishes and shared duties. These themes are not identical in every tale, and readers should look at the specific story rather than assume one message for all Turkic peoples.

A motif such as a journey, a wise elder, or a remarkable animal can appear in many storytelling traditions. Comparing how each tale uses a motif helps students notice both shared patterns and distinct cultural meanings.

Who tells the story?

In a performance tradition, the narrator is more than someone reading a fixed script. Voice, rhythm, music, gesture, and the response of listeners all help shape the event. A trained epic teller may learn from an experienced mentor, remember a long sequence, and adapt the delivery to an occasion.

When stories move into print or online, a new version can make choices about vocabulary, length, images, and audience. The site should name the source and explain when a text is an adaptation rather than a direct translation.

07 · English mythology, legend, and folklore

A changing tradition of heroes and wonder

There is no single ancient book of English mythology. The tradition grew through early English-language poetry, medieval legends, local folklore, and later retellings.

✦

From oral poetry to modern stories

Read each tale in its language and historical context: “English” stories often carry influences from across the British Isles and northern Europe.

BeowulfKing ArthurFolkloreRetellings

Beowulf and the Old English world

Beowulf is a major Old English poem. It tells of a hero who travels to help King Hrothgar and faces Grendel, Grendel’s mother, and later a dragon. Although the poem was written in England, its story is set among Scandinavian peoples in a distant past. Scholars do not know exactly when it was first composed; the surviving manuscript was copied around the year 1000.

The poem combines heroic values such as loyalty and courage with reflections on loss, memory, and the passing of kingdoms. It is a useful example of how an oral tradition can be reshaped when it is written into a manuscript.

Arthurian legend and medieval literature

The stories of King Arthur, Merlin, and the Knights of the Round Table developed over many centuries. They are part of a broader British and European literary tradition, not a simple historical biography. Medieval writers added new characters and episodes, and later authors reworked the stories for different audiences.

Arthurian tales often explore chivalry, leadership, loyalty, and the tension between personal desire and duty. Comparing two versions helps readers see how a legend changes with its audience.

Local folklore and later retellings

English folklore includes local legends, ballads, tales of extraordinary beings, and stories attached to particular landscapes. Robin Hood, for example, became a popular outlaw figure through ballads and later books and performances. These stories are not all from one period, and their details differ across versions.

For this site, each reading can include a short note on its setting, the source or version used, and the historical language level. Learners can then explore the English text, listen to an audio narration, and compare how storytellers shape the same legend.

“English mythology” is a useful project heading, but its stories have different origins. The examples below show how a poem, a royal legend, and community ballads each developed through a different path.

Reading the tradition · Context, character, and retelling

How to explore English legends thoughtfully

Beowulf: a hero in two stages of life

The poem has a clear arc. Beowulf first crosses the sea to help Hrothgar’s people, then defeats Grendel and Grendel’s mother. Much later, as an ageing king, he fights a dragon threatening his own people. His final victory comes at a cost: the poem ends with his funeral and a lament. That ending invites readers to think about a leader’s duty, the fragility of a community, and how a people remembers its heroes.

The surviving poem contains echoes of both an older pagan setting and Christian ideas. This layered perspective is one reason scholars continue to discuss how the poem was composed and shaped before its manuscript copy was made.

Arthur: legend built over time

Arthur is not presented in the same way by every writer. Geoffrey of Monmouth’s 12th-century history gave the story international reach, and later authors expanded the court, the knights, and their adventures. Places such as Tintagel acquired Arthurian associations through the legend, making landscape part of the storytelling.

Readers can separate three questions: what a medieval writer claimed, what evidence historians can check, and what later storytellers imagined. This makes the difference between legend and historical evidence easier to understand.
